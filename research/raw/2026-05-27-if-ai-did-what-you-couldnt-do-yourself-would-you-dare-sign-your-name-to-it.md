# If AI Did What You Couldn’t Do Yourself, Would You Dare Sign Your Name to It?

### When you use AI to do things, are you the verifier or the delegator?

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*ZmExwQygXF7G66Sj.jpg)

At last week’s group meeting, after a student finished presenting an adjusted thesis-proposal direction, a sentence suddenly popped into my head:

**AI provides capability, but responsibility has to be borne by the author. This is the greatest tension in human-AI collaboration.**

I didn’t read this line in some book. I distilled it myself during the group discussion. At the time, we were talking about verification in human-AI collaboration. As the conversation went on, I suddenly realized that the whole issue actually begins with a more fundamental question: **the moment you outsource cognition to AI, you lose the ability to verify it.**

This isn’t a matter of attitude — not that you aren’t careful enough or responsible enough. It’s a matter of ability — you simply can’t check it.

Think about it. You ask AI to do a data analysis for you. It produces a result; you look at it and think, “Hmm, that seems reasonable.” But what does “seems reasonable” mean? It means **it matches your intuitive expectations**. And your intuitive expectations are precisely the boundary of the cognitive work you couldn’t do yourself and therefore outsourced to AI. You are using a level of cognition you don’t possess to judge an analysis you couldn’t have produced — that isn’t verification. That’s **rubber-stamping**.

What makes this even more painful is that the student’s original proposal direction targeted people with high AI literacy. At the group meeting, I reminded him: “You’ve spent too much time in capable AI environments. You’ll forget what ordinary people’s actual cognitive level is like.” He uses AI every day, so he thinks verification isn’t hard; he thinks “just look at it carefully” is enough. But what I want to say is this: those of us who spend every day immersed in AI environments have already become familiar with AI’s output patterns. When we see a result, we can roughly tell where problems may lie. But ordinary people? When they receive an AI-generated report, what can they do?

Honestly, very little.

You may think this is just my personal overcaution about AI. But look at what has happened in other domains. In 2009, a Turkish Airlines flight crashed while landing in Amsterdam, killing nine people. What happened? Because of a sensor failure, the aircraft’s autopilot system mistakenly judged that the plane had already reached the ground and automatically entered landing flare mode — slowing down and pitching the nose up. There was fog at the time, and the pilots had difficulty judging the true altitude visually, so they could only rely on instrument readings. The system said everything was fine, so they believed it. They did not discover that the speed was too low until around 500 feet, but by then it was too late. The pilots were not careless. They were rigorously trained professional pilots. But once an automated system gives a signal, the human brain subconsciously chooses the path of least effort: believe it. In psychology, this is called “automation bias” ([Parasuraman & Riley, 1997](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872097778543886)) — not an attitude problem, but a problem of cognitive structure.

If even pilots are susceptible to this, what can ordinary people do?

**AI does something for you that you couldn’t do yourself, then asks you to sign off and take responsibility — but the basis for your signature is precisely the part of cognition you don’t have. This is not collaboration. It is inversion.**

## The Predicament

At this point, you may think: then I’ll just check more carefully, spend more time on it, and surely I can find the problems.

But the issue is this: this is not merely your personal problem. It is a structural predicament.

What is a structural predicament? It is the kind of problem you cannot solve by changing your attitude or your method. The root of the problem is not in you, but in the very structure of human-AI collaboration.

Consider an everyday scenario: you use GPS navigation to drive to an unfamiliar place. When you arrive, you turn off the navigation — and then do you know how to get back?

Most people don’t. GPS has made the directional judgments for you; all you need to do is follow along. Very convenient. But what is the cost of that convenience? Your spatial cognition has been outsourced — your sense of direction has not disappeared, but it is atrophying. Bohbot and colleagues found that people who use GPS more frequently have worse hippocampus-dependent spatial memory when GPS assistance is unavailable, and a three-year follow-up found an even steeper decline ([Bohbot et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62877-0)). This is not “being too lazy to look at the road.” It is **an outsourced ability atrophying — and the ability to verify whether the navigation is correct atrophying along with it**.

See how this logic is exactly the same as with AI? When you use GPS navigation, directional judgment is outsourced, spatial cognition slowly atrophies, and when the navigation makes a mistake, you can’t detect it. When you use AI for analysis, cognitive judgment is outsourced, verification ability slowly atrophies, and when AI makes a mistake, you can’t detect it either. Same structure, different domain.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*CDynKt-Iwt2TaZIb.jpg)

When I reminded the student of that point, there was another layer of meaning I didn’t say out loud: **the moment you outsource cognition, what you outsource is not merely one specific ability, but the entire cognitive chain.** From understanding to memory to judgment to doubt — you no longer understand for yourself, because AI has summarized it for you; you no longer remember for yourself, because AI has stored it for you; you no longer judge for yourself, because AI has analyzed it for you; you no longer question for yourself, because AI’s output looks reasonable enough. The entire chain is outsourced. After that, what do you have left with which to verify anything?

A more accurate statement is not “AI makes people lazy,” but rather: **AI may rapidly raise a person’s ability to produce outputs, but it does not necessarily raise their ability to judge those outputs at the same pace.** These two things are very different. Output ability is whether you can produce a plan, report, or diagnostic suggestion that looks decent. Judgment ability is whether you know what assumptions the plan rests on, where it might be wrong, and what evidence should make you change your mind.

AI raises the former. But the latter — you have to cultivate and refine it yourself. And cultivating judgment is precisely the thing you stop doing once you outsource cognition.

This is the real predicament: it isn’t that you don’t want to check. It’s that you can’t. It isn’t that you aren’t responsible enough. It’s that the basis on which you could take responsibility has already been outsourced.

## The Misconception

Since the predicament is structural, simple answers definitely won’t be enough. So what are the common simple answers?

“Ask AI to explain itself.”

This intuition has something reasonable in it. An explanation is of course better than no explanation. AI tells you how it reasoned; you follow its logic once and feel that it makes sense. But “having an explanation” is not the same as “a human being can judge it.”

Here’s an analogy: you hire a chef to cook a dish. The chef tells you what ingredients he used, how he seasoned it, and how he controlled the heat. You have the ingredient list, and you understand the cooking steps — but being told the steps does not mean you can judge whether each step was done correctly. Was the heat too high? Was the seasoning ratio appropriate? You don’t know. Because you don’t know how to cook. You can only look at the final dish when it is served; you cannot verify the process in between.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*IhIO1H_zvHdUi_Bv.jpg)

A 2024 study in *Nature Scientific Reports*, involving five experiments and 1,403 participants, found that making AI predictions more explainable **does not reduce people’s overreliance on incorrect advice** ([Cecil et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-60220-5)). Explanations may help you better understand the AI’s reasoning — “it does sound reasonable” — but understanding its reasoning and judging whether its reasoning is correct are two different things. An explanation may make you rely on it more, not verify it better.

Toby Walsh gives a sharp example in *Machines Behaving Badly*: if an AI system transparently explains to you that it cannot hire you because you are a woman, what value does that have? Transparently telling you how it reasoned is not the same as giving you the ability to verify whether its reasoning is correct. **Transparency is information disclosure; verification is ability matching.** These are very different things.

You see, “check more carefully” doesn’t work, and “ask AI to explain” doesn’t work either. Both common answers assume the same premise: that you have the ability to verify, and only need methodological guidance. But our core predicament is precisely this: **the problem is not method, but a systematic mismatch between ability and responsibility.** You have responsibility but not ability. Method cannot solve an ability problem — just as giving a person who cannot cook a more detailed recipe still won’t let them judge whether each step was done correctly.

## The Opening

So where is the way out?

Since insufficient ability is a fact, the way out is not to “make up the ability” — that takes too long, and the ability you have outsourced is still atrophying. The way out should be: **lower the threshold. Bring the threshold for verification back within the range of human ability.**

This is the core idea of asymmetric verification.

What is asymmetric verification? Here’s an analogy: a food critic doesn’t need to know how to cook, but can still judge the quality of a dish. A goalkeeper doesn’t need to play the whole match; he only needs to guard the goal. Verification does not require repeating the whole process; it only requires checking key nodes.

Food critics and goalkeepers do something asymmetrical to what producers do. A chef must master heat control, knife work, and ingredient pairing — these are production abilities. A food critic only needs to judge: does this dish taste good, are the ingredients fresh, is the seasoning reasonable? A goalkeeper does not need to know how the striker dribbles or how the midfielder passes; he only needs to judge: should this ball be saved? **The ability required of the verifier and the ability required of the producer are asymmetrical.**

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*6A4E2Tis2EExe8T0.jpg)

This logic is actually reflected in many real-world domains. Food-safety spot checks do not require testing every item of food; they only require sampling at key nodes — and retesting must be performed by a different institution, not by the producer checking itself. Huawei has a practice in information verification called “asymmetric confirmation”: when its information management office and employees verify information, they do not verify it with the employee themself, but cross-check it one by one with the employee’s former supervisors and previous deputy representatives (from *Huawei Has No Secrets*). The verifier and the verified party have asymmetric information, but the verifier guards the key nodes through cross-validation.

Back to AI. If you ask AI to do a data analysis, you do not need to manually rerun the entire process yourself — you don’t have the ability, and you don’t have the time. But you can verify at key nodes: Are the data sources AI used reliable? What are its core assumptions? If the conclusion is wrong, where is it most likely to be wrong? You don’t need to repeat AI’s calculations to answer these questions. You only need to use your own judgment to check a few critical positions. If the check passes, move on. If it doesn’t, stop and ask further questions.

This is the gatekeeper mindset: **you don’t need to understand more than AI does. You only need to be confident at key nodes when you say, “I accept this” or “I don’t accept this.”**

But what if you cannot judge even the key nodes? Then there is another option: **let another AI help you guard the gate.** Not by asking AI to explain itself (we already know explanations don’t solve the problem), but by asking a different AI, using different logic and different angles, to cross-validate key nodes. Just as food-safety retesting must be performed by a different institution, the verifier and the producer cannot be the same.

Of course, there is one premise that must be preserved: **the final signature must be human.** AI can help lower the threshold for verification, but judgment, taste, and responsibility — these three things cannot be handed over. A goalkeeper may use tools to judge the trajectory of the ball, but the final save must be his own decision.

## Verification

After saying all this, have I done it myself?

To be honest, I’m working on it. The core tension distilled at the group meeting — AI provides capability, but responsibility must be borne by the author — has kept pushing me to think more deeply. Let me describe a more concrete personal experience.

Some time ago, [I conducted an AI-assisted cold-start experiment for Zettelkasten (the card-note method)](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/w51HIB0kCNgUlEpYGcZPjg). I had more than 2,000 notes and wanted to turn them into a network of linked cards — identifying hidden relationships among notes and forming a bottom-up knowledge network. The workload was enormous. I couldn’t do it myself, so I asked AI to do it.

AI was indeed fast. In 16 hours, it processed more than 2,000 notes for me, linking and tagging them. But those 16 hours consumed 50% of my weekly token quota. What does that mean? It means I could not run it repeatedly, and I could not verify everything from scratch each time. I had to verify key nodes within limited resources.

How did I verify it? Honestly, I did less than I had hoped.

The notes form a bottom-up, networked structure without categories — so there is no question of whether a category system is internally consistent. Some clusters had already formed among the notes — semantically similar notes naturally gathered together. But for connections between clusters, AI randomly drew two cards to see whether some kind of long-range connection could emerge between them, thereby linking two large clusters. With this kind of connection, you can only check whether the connections it gives you make sense; you cannot determine whether “all the connections that should exist are there” — because if you could determine that, you would not need AI to help you discover them.

What I could truly do was only one thing: **check whether the direction of the connection was right.** When AI told me note A and note B were related, I did not need to verify how it calculated that. I only needed to judge: are these two notes truly related? My domain knowledge could answer that question. Most of the connections were right, but there were indeed a few cases where AI forced unrelated notes together — they looked connected on the surface, but after closer thought, they were not the same thing. I found these errors during key-node verification.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*QyVQes7Ra8Et2LsY.png)

You see, this is how asymmetric verification works in a real scenario. I did not rerun the entire process, and I did not understand every possible relationship among the notes better than AI did — with more than 2,000 notes, there is no way I could remember each one. But at a few key positions, I had the confidence to say “I accept this” or “I don’t accept this,” and that was enough.

Of course, I also have to admit that this experiment made the boundaries of asymmetric verification clearer to me. First, my domain knowledge helped — these were notes I had written myself, so I had judgment about the content. If they were someone else’s notes, my verification ability would decline significantly. Second, selecting the key nodes itself requires experience — knowing where to check is itself a form of judgment, and not everyone has it at the beginning. Third, with some connections, you can only verify “the parts that were given,” not “the parts that were omitted.” The value of serendipity is precisely that it discovers connections you had not thought of. If you could already judge “where connections should exist,” you would not need serendipity.

But the direction is right. Asymmetric verification does not require you to know more than AI. It only requires you to exercise judgment at key nodes. And selecting those key nodes is something that can be learned — just as a food critic is not born knowing how to taste; they also need training.

## Discussion

Asymmetric verification lowers the threshold, but it does not lower the threshold to zero. At minimum, you need to know what the key assumptions in the domain are, what kinds of results are “unreasonable,” and in which direction to question when something goes wrong. Knowing where to verify is itself a form of judgment.

So where is the lower bound of ability?

The lower bound is whether you can clearly explain these things. If you can, you are verifying — you are a collaborator. If you cannot, you are endorsing — you are a delegator.

This leads to the distinction I think is most worth clarifying: **the boundary between collaboration and delegation.**

Truly good collaboration does not require humans to always know more than AI. Humans need to be able to determine value goals, understand key assumptions, identify failure conditions, set stop-loss plans, and know when a more specialized person must be brought in. If you can roughly explain these questions, then we can continue to talk about collaboration. If you cannot explain them at all, that does not mean you cannot use AI. It just means you should be honest: this is not collaboration, but **delegation**.

Delegation is not a bad thing. You delegate litigation to a lawyer; you delegate tax filing to an accountant. These are all normal. But delegation rests on one premise: you know that you have delegated, and you acknowledge that in the position where you sign your name, you are not the verifier, but the endorser. You cannot pretend that you are verifying — that is just rubber-stamping.

In delegation scenarios, two specific issues are worth noting.

The first is the paradox of serendipity. My Zettelkasten experiment exposed this paradox: the connections AI discovers — the ones you had not thought of — are precisely the parts you cannot verify. You can only check whether the connections it gives you “make sense,” but you cannot verify whether “all the places where connections should exist actually have connections.” Because if you could judge where connections should exist, you would not need AI to help you discover them.

This means AI outputs actually come in two kinds. One kind is reasoned out — data analysis, plan design, report writing — which you can verify through key-node checks. The other kind is discovered — new connections, new insights — which you cannot verify through calculation checks, because the essence of discovery is that “you hadn’t thought of it.” For this kind of output, the method is not verification, but **trial use**. Is this connection useful to me? Can it inspire new thinking? If it is useful, keep it; if not, let it go. This is not verification, but filtering — and filtering is also a form of judgment.

The second issue is the dependency loop of using AI to verify AI. You might say: use AI to solve the pit AI dug — let another AI help you verify key nodes. But do you also need to verify the output of that verification AI? If so, haven’t we returned to the original predicament?

No. Because the task of the verification AI is much simpler than the task of the production AI. The production AI has to complete the whole process; the verification AI only has to check key nodes. The threshold for verification is lower than the threshold for production, so the verification AI’s output is easier for a human to judge. You do not need to find a third AI to verify the second AI — because what the second AI is doing has already been brought within your range of ability.

It’s like this: you don’t know how to cook, and your judgment of whether a dish is good is only rough — you can tell if it is too salty, but you cannot say whether the seasoning ratio is optimal. So you ask a food critic to help you judge. Can you accept the critic’s judgment? Yes, because what the critic does (tasting) is closer to your ability range than what the chef does (cooking). You do not need to find someone else to verify the critic.

## Conclusion

After the group meeting ended, on the way back I kept thinking about one question: if I had to give one piece of advice to people using AI to do things today, what would I say?

I think I would say: **first, figure out what position you are in.**

Are you the verifier, or the delegator? If you can roughly explain what the key assumptions are, what kinds of results would be unreasonable, and in which direction to question when something goes wrong, then you are collaborating, and asymmetric verification can help lower the threshold. If you cannot explain any of these things, then you are delegating. Be honest and admit that you are endorsing, not verifying.

Then, **verify at the key nodes.** You do not need to repeat the entire process, and you do not need to understand more than AI does. You only need to be confident at a few critical positions when you say, “I accept this” or “I don’t accept this.” If the check passes, move on. If it doesn’t, stop and ask further questions. Selecting key nodes can be learned — just as a food critic is not born knowing how to taste.

Finally, **judgment, taste, and responsibility — these three things cannot be handed over.** AI can help you lower the threshold for verification, help you check key nodes, and help you discover connections you had not thought of. But the final signature must be yours. A goalkeeper may use tools to judge the trajectory of the ball, but the moment of making the save must be his own decision.

You see, the greatest tension in human-AI collaboration — AI provides capability, but responsibility must be borne by the author — is not without a way out. The way out is not to make up the ability (too slow), nor to check more carefully (not feasible), but to lower the threshold: bring the threshold for verification back within the range of human ability. The gatekeeper does not need to play the whole match; he only needs to guard the goal.

But the premise of guarding the goal is that you must be able to judge whether the ball should be saved. If you cannot even judge that, then you are not a goalkeeper. You are merely someone standing in front of the goal.
