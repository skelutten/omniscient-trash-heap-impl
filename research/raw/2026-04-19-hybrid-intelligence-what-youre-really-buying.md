# Hybrid Intelligence: What You’re Really Buying

### Hybrid intelligence isn’t some future trend; it’s the reality we’re already living in.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/0*S1NxdCN2H1CI3L29.png)

## The Blend

A friend sent me a screenshot the other day: “This platform claims their latest flagship GPT API costs just a few bucks per million tokens — is that legit?”

One look gave me the answer. Sure, you’ll get some genuine flagship GPT calls — but not all of them. Not even close. Quick aside: a token is the basic unit large language models bill by. The official flagship API’s input price works out to roughly a dozen yuan per million tokens; when a reseller is 10× cheaper than that, the product has almost certainly been watered down.

There are already [documented cases](https://www.anthropic.com/news/detecting-and-preventing-distillation-attacks) of exactly this — API resellers advertising a top-tier model, while only a small fraction of the requests actually reach the model they promised. The rest get quietly routed to cheaper or open-source models and blended into what streams back to you. What you see on your screen is one smooth reply after another; what you can’t tell is that one sentence came from the flagship, and the next from some small open-source model that bills pennies per million tokens.

I’m not here to moralize about that today. I want you to see the structure first: multiple sources of intelligence mixed into a single stream, handed to you as one product, with no way to tell them apart.

The structure itself is nothing new. Food delivery has ghost kitchens. Fashion has white-label manufacturing. That “single-origin” pour-over at your local café may well be cut with a few other beans.

The catch is that this same structure is now moving — faster than most of us have noticed — into a domain that once seemed immune: the expertise services humans sell.

## Putting a Name On It

There’s already a term for this: hybrid intelligence.

Academia has its own vocabulary here — [human-in-the-loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human-in-the-loop) frames it as a process, and hybrid intelligence frames it as a system-design problem. What I want to talk about is neither. I want to look at it from the buyer’s side: you’re paying for what looks like human expertise, but what’s inside is a mix of human and AI.

Let me spell out that this mixing happens on three layers, all of which come up later: first, the **ingredient layer** — the final output contains both human work and AI work; second, the **process layer** — humans and AI collaborate while the work is being made; third, the **byline layer** — the product carries one person’s name, but the work behind it is a human-machine collaboration.

In this mix, the human brings judgment, taste, and the name on the tin; AI handles the labor-intensive, formulaic pieces — the grunt work nobody notices but somebody has to do.

Which means: going forward, all-natural, AI-free expertise is going to be rare; most knowledge services will have AI woven into them in one form or another.

That stings a little, but I don’t mean it as pessimism — just as naming something that’s already happening.

You hire a tutor, and behind the scenes AI may be helping the teacher put together problem sets and draft explanations. You see a doctor, and the intake questions, the chart notes, and the background reading may all involve AI. You book a consulting session, and before you even walk in, the consultant has probably had AI comb through your industry, your company, and your role — and the analysis you hear during that half-hour in the room is itself a blended product.

This isn’t moral decay. It’s a structural reshuffle — any expertise service you can split into “needs a human” and “doesn’t need a human” parts will see AI fill the second bucket, fast. The only question is how fast — and that depends on how finely you can slice the work.

## A Case in Point: My Slides

I don’t want to turn this essay into a prediction about the future. Hybrid intelligence isn’t the distant future — it’s already here, and I’m already doing it.

As a university instructor, I lecture every week. Lecturing means slides. Since 2014, I’ve used [reveal.js](https://revealjs.com/) for minimalist outline-style slides — no illustrations, no polished animations. Earlier this year I entered the [National Teaching Innovation Contest for College Instructors](https://nticct.cahe.edu.cn/), and the judges looked at my plain slides and said, politely but clearly: visually too thin. So over winter break I built myself a fresh AI-assisted slide-making workflow.

I’ve already written about how I built it — see [AI-made slides look slick, but are you still in control?](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/d7LAxs3bSnmfAlP8AMSkSA) — so I won’t rehash that. Today let’s flip to the consumer side of the classroom: what the students actually experience.

Since the spring term started, I’ve been using the new slides in class. In a 45-minute session, I’m lecturing up front while the screen carries illustrated infographics, evenly paced animations, and visuals far sharper than anything I used to make. Students follow with their eyes on the screen and their ears on me. After class, a few come up and ask, almost shyly, “Can you send us the slides?” — a sure sign the decks are landing. Not one student asks, “Did you make this by hand, or did AI draw it?” The question doesn’t matter to them.

And it isn’t just my class — one of my grad students learned the workflow and put together a deck for a class presentation. The instructor and classmates watching were impressed. What were they judging during those ten or fifteen minutes? The presentation as a whole — whether the argument held together, whether the visuals served the content, whether the delivery drew people in. Nobody sits there slide by slide asking who drew which image or which model made which animation. That’s not something the audience needs to care about.

What a student takes from a class, and what a teacher and classmates see in a presentation, is a finished piece of work. For the student, the point is learning what the lesson actually teaches — and being able to use it on homework, exams, and projects. For the instructor watching a talk, the point is whether that talk has value — whether you can see that the student has really absorbed the material.

How the human and the AI split the work behind the scenes doesn’t change what the user actually needs. What they need is something they can use, something with a point of view — and, above all, a piece of work that carries your name.

That’s why I’m happy to let AI into my slides — not to cut corners, but because what matters to the audience is a piece of work that carries my taste and my judgment.

## The Mix

Step out of the classroom and look at the knowledge market more broadly — it gets more interesting.

You go to a lecture by a well-known speaker. They’re really there, on stage; the voice, the expressions, the live response are all real. But the examples in the talk may have been pulled out of their back catalog by AI, and the takeaway notes you leave with may have been compiled by AI. The human part is concentrated in the one or two hours you’re in the room. Every other stage where intelligent output could be blended already has been.

Same story when you subscribe to a well-known writer’s weekly column, delivered like clockwork. How many of those did they actually draft themselves, sitting in a café? How many came from feeding a prompt into their own workflow, nudging a few sentences, and hitting publish? You don’t know. And as the models get better, you’ll have even fewer reliable ways to tell.

The point I want to make isn’t that you’ve been cheated. To be cheated, someone has to have promised you something **all-handmade** in the first place. That was the default assumption about expert services for a long time — but it was really just consumer inertia. You assume that if someone’s name is on the work, they **made the whole thing themselves**, start to finish.

That default is breaking down. Not because providers have gone crooked, but because they too have to use AI — to stay competitive and not burn out. Your weekly columnist has to eat, sleep, and raise kids. If they can’t find a way to make hybrid intelligence work for them, they won’t keep up with today’s pace — let alone the faster one coming.

The ones who can’t keep up drop out. The ones who can survive — especially in high-cadence formats like columns, weekly podcasts, and short video. Which means the work you’ll keep seeing from these big names will, more and more, be a hybrid-intelligence product.

So the question isn’t “are there still fully human services?” Services with a high share of genuine human judgment do still exist — top-tier bespoke work, six-figure consultations, closed-door sessions — and in those settings the **weight of human judgment** is still high, because the price lets one consultant keep their client list small. They may use AI for research or first drafts, but the judgments they deliver face-to-face still come from their own experience.

The question is what happens further down-market. The columns, courses, and talks most of us actually consume — the ones with the biggest reach. At that level, the human content gets thinner and thinner. Not missing, but thin enough that you have to rethink what you’re actually paying for.

What you’re buying is no longer handmade. It’s more like a cup of blended coffee — not single-origin, but mixed from several beans, served under one name.

## Living With It

This sounds like bad news, but it isn’t. What actually matters hasn’t changed — we just weren’t paying attention to it before.

Since the blending isn’t going away, the question to ask isn’t how much of this is human. It’s how good the finished work is. In one word: quality.

What counts as good quality? Look at both sides of the transaction.

On the human side: look at what they’ve kept in their own hands. **Judgment, taste, and responsibility.** Those three can’t be outsourced.

Take my slides. Judgment: what I cover first, what comes next — that has to be my call. AI can’t help, because it doesn’t know where last week’s session left off or where the students are stuck right now. Taste: how slow the animation on a slide has to be so students don’t lose the thread; which image, the moment it lands on screen, will kill the mood. That comes from years of standing at a podium. Responsibility: if a call I made in lecture is wrong and the students learn it wrong, I’m the one on the hook, not AI — when I sign the grade roster, I’m signing for every judgment behind the semester. Hold on to those three, and the lecture is mine — no matter how many models touched the work along the way.

On the AI side, look at whether it was used well. Some people use AI to get by — crank out whatever, slap their name on it, ship it, done. That’s a short-term fix that rots the foundation, and **I’m not criticizing hybrid intelligence itself** — I’m criticizing that irresponsible move: dropping the judgment, taste, and responsibility you were supposed to keep, leaving only your name on the work. That burns your credibility sooner or later. It’s very different from using AI to offload repetitive work so you can spend more time on what only you can do. That approach builds credibility.

As a consumer, the skill you need isn’t a reflex for spotting “which part was AI.” That kind of spotting will only get harder as AI improves. In an era where hybrid intelligence is here to stay, the real skill to cultivate is the taste and judgment to tell good work from bad.

How? Three things to watch for:

First, **do they offer concrete judgments AI couldn’t have produced** — counterintuitive conclusions, numbers only someone in the room would know, the split-second call that only comes from being there? AI can’t make those up, because it wasn’t in the room.

Second, **do their recent pieces hold a consistent stance, and evolve in a way that makes sense?** A real person has a through-line; their views shift gradually. AI-assembled pieces each look polished on their own, but across a run of articles you miss the sense of someone actually thinking things through.

Third, **their first reaction to a breaking event** — does it click into the worldview they’ve already shown you? AI usually handles breaking news by summarizing and offering a mild take. A real person slots the event into what they already think — and the response has an edge.

A lecture with real teaching rhythm versus one that’s pure visual flash — students can tell. An article with judgment versus a pile of information — readers feel the difference. A consultation grounded in real experience versus one phoned in with AI as ghostwriter — the client knows.

## The Bottom Line

We used to pay a premium for “pure handmade,” figuring that if someone typed the words and drew the images themselves, that was proof of sincerity and value. Now technology has dismantled that habit. It’s forcing us, as consumers, to finish an overdue disenchantment — to stop paying blindly for the effort of the process and start paying for the value of the result.

Hybrid intelligence isn’t some future trend; it’s the reality we’re already living in. In this reality, the grown-up move isn’t to pick up a magnifying glass and hunt for AI fingerprints — it’s to be a clear-eyed judge of the work itself.

As long as the person whose name is on it still keeps their judgment, taste, and sense of responsibility in the work — as the thing they refuse to give up — that blended cup of coffee is still worth picking up and savoring.

So pour yourself a cup.
